\section{Noise Contributions}
\label{sec:performance}
We estimate the noise after TDI and clock correction by propagating measured electronic and modulation spectra together with a calculated detector contribution. For comparison, we use the optical-metrology and test-mass acceleration reference adopted by Ferguson et al.~\cite{ferguson2025applying}. The following calculation identifies the relative contributions of these sources; it assumes matched physical and processing delays.

We use the measured electronic delay-line baseline as an equivalent noise contribution for each carrier and sideband readout. The same ASD is assigned to every link and channel, with independent noise realizations. This describes the measured electronic chain without separating its individual components.

The delay boards share a timing reference. We write $\epsilon_i=\epsilon_{\mathrm{com}}+\Delta\epsilon_i$ and combine all appearances of $\epsilon_{\mathrm{com}}$ before calculating its noise power. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, TDI with clock correction suppresses this common term to the same order as laser noise. At the retained cancellation order, the board-timing residual is therefore
\begin{equation}
 X_{2\mathrm c}^{\epsilon}=\sum_i T_{\Delta\epsilon_i}\Delta\epsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $T_{\Delta\epsilon_i}$ denotes the complete delay response of the differential timing error on board $i$. The spectrum of the shared reference is not an input spectrum for these differential terms.

We assume differential timing to be small compared with the included electronic and modulation contributions and neglect it in the plotted sum. This describes the shared-reference configuration; it does not assign an independent oscillator spectrum to each board. The additional coupling of delay fluctuations to laser-frequency noise and residuals from imperfect delay implementation are also excluded.

\subsection{Propagation Through TDI}
\label{sec:noise_propagation}
We follow the noise-propagation approach of Hartwig and Bayle~\cite{hartwig_clock-jitter_2021}. First, each noise source is written in the carrier and sideband $\eta$ measurements. We then form the clock-transfer variables and substitute the measurements into the corrected Michelson combination. Collecting all appearances of one source gives its complete delay polynomial and hence its transfer function. This includes both the physical link delays and the delays applied in processing.

Let $T_a(f)$ be the transfer function of source $n_a$, with PSD $S_a(f)$. For independent sources, the output PSD is
\begin{equation}
 S_X(f)=\sum_a |T_a(f)|^2 S_a(f).
\end{equation}
The order of these operations matters. A carrier readout reused in clock correction is the same noise source in both occurrences, so its coefficients must be added before taking the squared magnitude. The same applies to a clock fluctuation shared between boards. Treating these occurrences as independent would remove the cancellations present in the measurement model.

For changing arms, the transfer functions also depend on the evaluation time. We calculate each response using the delays at that time, including the earlier times reached along a sequence of delays, and average the resulting noise powers over the chosen interval. This approximates the spectrum while the arms vary slowly. Appendix~\ref{app:noise_parameters} specifies the delay model, averaging interval and checks on the calculation.

\subsection{Combined Noise Contributions and Implications}
Figure~\ref{fig:total_tdi2_noise} shows the propagated electronic, modulation and detector contributions. The black curve is their quadrature sum, under the assumption that differential board timing is negligible. The dashed reference combines optical-metrology and test-mass acceleration noise, converted to phase at 1064 nm and propagated as independent equivalent link noises. Its input scales are $15\,\mathrm{pm}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ and $3\,\mathrm{fm\,s^{-2}}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$, with the frequency dependence given in \cite{colpi_lisa_2024}. Test-mass noise is included only in this reference.

The detector contribution lies well below the electronic and modulation curves. The bright optical readout therefore does not set the calculated residual. Among the contributions based on measured spectra, the electronic baseline is larger than the modulation estimate over most of the plotted band. Under the direct-phase-noise assumption, improving the modulation source alone would consequently leave the electronic contribution as the main limitation. This conclusion concerns the present operating point and the measured estimator; it does not establish the noise of the planned Moku modulation chain.

This conclusion assumes that differential board timing remains below the included contributions. The shared reference is combined coherently in the measurement model and does not enter as an independent board-noise floor.

The repeated minima at higher frequencies arise from the delayed differences in the TDI response. They occur in several component curves and in the propagated reference. A small ASD at one of these minima is therefore not evidence of a quieter instrument; a sensitivity comparison must also include the response to the injected GW signal. The dashed curve provides a common noise scale, but crossing below it does not by itself demonstrate LISA-like sensitivity or successful laser- and clock-noise suppression.

The calculation thus supports using strong optical beats to study noise cancellation without an evident detector-noise limitation. The next useful improvements are to establish the differential board-timing contribution and reduce or characterize the electronic residual, while repeating the modulation measurement with the intended source and readout. Once delay-related residuals are included, the resulting budget can be tested against the assembled system and the recovery of a specified GW injection.
