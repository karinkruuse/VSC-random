\section{Noise Contributions}
\label{sec:performance}
We estimate the noise after TDI and clock correction by propagating measured electronic and modulation spectra together with a calculated detector contribution. For comparison, we use the optical-metrology and test-mass acceleration reference adopted by Ferguson et al.~\cite{ferguson2025applying}. The following calculation identifies the relative contributions of these sources; it assumes matched physical and processing delays.

\subsection{Modulation and Optical Readout Noise}
\label{sec:readout_noise}
Modulation noise and optical readout noise enter the measurement at different stages. Modulation noise is added while generating the sidebands used for clock transfer, as in LISA. Readout noise is added when measuring the carrier and sideband phases; it contributes to both the science measurement and clock correction.

We estimate modulation noise from half the phase difference between the upper and lower sidebands of a local optical beat. The common optical phase cancels, leaving modulation fluctuations and readout noise. We assign this measured spectrum to the direct modulation phase term, $m_i=\psi_i^{\mathrm{mod}}$, with $\tau_i^{\mathrm{mod}}=0$. This assignment does not separate modulator noise from noise in the measurement itself.

Separately, the detector calculation gives carrier and sideband readout ASDs of approximately $3\times10^{-9}$ and $1\times10^{-8}$ cycles/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ for the assumed 4 mW per beam. These estimates lie well below the measured modulation and electronic spectra, indicating that optical readout should not limit this operating point. The relevant difference from LISA is the received optical power: miniLISA does not reproduce the weak received inter-spacecraft beam.

The modulation measurement already contains some readout noise, so adding the detector estimate separately can count a small contribution twice. The resulting sum is a component estimate, with this ambiguity well below the larger contributions.

\subsection{Electronic Noise and Board Timing}
\label{sec:electronic_noise}
The delay boards contribute additive electronic noise and timing fluctuations. We treat these separately: the measured baseline represents additive phase noise, while board timing couples through the input and output frequencies and the programmed delays.

We use the electronic delay-line baseline measured by Ferguson et al.~\cite{ferguson2025applying} as an equivalent noise contribution for each carrier and sideband readout. The same ASD is assigned to every link and channel, with independent noise realizations. The baseline describes the measured chain without resolving its individual electronic components.

For timing, we write $\epsilon_i=\epsilon_{\mathrm{com}}+\Delta\epsilon_i$, separating the shared reference from differences between boards. All appearances of the shared term are combined before calculating its noise power. In the constant-frequency model with matched delays, its residual has the same delay operator as laser phase noise. Thus the ideal TDI cancellation also applies to this term; it does not imply equal residual amplitudes for timing and laser noise. Residual power depends on the input spectrum, and a mismatch between physical and processing delays introduces additional leakage.

At the cancellation order retained here, the remaining board-timing contribution is
\begin{equation}
 X_{2\mathrm c}^{\epsilon}=\sum_i T_{\Delta\epsilon_i}\Delta\epsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $T_{\Delta\epsilon_i}$ is the complete delay response of the differential timing error on board $i$. The shared reference spectrum is not the spectrum of these differential terms.

We assume differential timing is below the electronic and modulation contributions and omit it from the plotted sum. Coupling of delay fluctuations to laser-frequency noise and errors in the implemented delays are also excluded; their size requires a measurement of the timing and delay residuals.

\subsection{Propagation Through TDI}
\label{sec:noise_propagation}
We follow the noise-propagation approach of Hartwig and Bayle~\cite{hartwig_clock-jitter_2021}. First, each noise source is written in the carrier and sideband $\eta$ measurements. We then form the clock-transfer variables and substitute the measurements into the corrected Michelson combination. Collecting all appearances of one source gives its complete delay polynomial and hence its transfer function. This includes both the physical link delays and the delays applied in processing.

Let $T_a(f)$ be the transfer function of source $n_a$, with PSD $S_a(f)$. For independent sources, the output PSD is
\begin{equation}
 S_X(f)=\sum_a |T_a(f)|^2 S_a(f).
\end{equation}
The order of these operations matters. A carrier readout reused in clock correction is the same noise source in both occurrences, so its coefficients must be added before taking the squared magnitude. The same applies to a clock fluctuation shared between boards. Treating these occurrences as independent would remove the cancellations present in the measurement model.

For changing arms, the transfer functions also depend on the evaluation time. We calculate each response using the delays at that time, including the earlier times reached along a sequence of delays, and average the resulting noise powers over the chosen interval. This approximates the spectrum while the arms vary slowly. Appendix~\ref{app:noise_parameters} specifies the delay model, averaging interval and checks on the calculation.

\subsection{Combined Noise Contributions and Implications}
Figure~\ref{fig:total_tdi2_noise} shows the propagated electronic, modulation and detector contributions. The black curve is their quadrature sum, under the assumption that differential board timing is negligible. The dashed reference combines optical-metrology and test-mass acceleration noise, converted to phase at 1064 nm and propagated as independent equivalent link noises. Its input scales are $15\,\mathrm{pm}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ and $3\,\mathrm{fm\,s^{-2}}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$, with the frequency dependence given in \cite{colpi_lisa_2024}. Test-mass noise is included only in this reference.

The detector contribution lies well below the electronic and modulation curves. Optical readout therefore does not set the calculated residual at the assumed powers. Among the contributions based on measured spectra, the electronic baseline is larger than the modulation estimate over most of the plotted band. Under the direct-phase-noise assumption, improving the modulation source alone would consequently leave the electronic contribution as the main limitation. This conclusion concerns the present operating point and the measured estimator; it does not establish the noise of the planned Moku modulation chain.

This conclusion assumes that differential board timing remains below the included contributions. The shared reference is combined coherently in the measurement model and does not enter as an independent board-noise floor.

The repeated minima at higher frequencies arise from the delayed differences in the TDI response. They occur in several component curves and in the propagated reference. A small ASD at one of these minima is therefore not evidence of a quieter instrument; a sensitivity comparison must also include the response to the injected GW signal. The dashed curve provides a common noise scale, but crossing below it does not by itself demonstrate LISA-like sensitivity or successful laser- and clock-noise suppression.

The calculation therefore indicates that noise cancellation can be studied at the assumed optical powers without a detector-noise limitation. The next useful improvements are to establish the differential board-timing contribution and reduce or characterize the electronic residual, while repeating the modulation measurement with the intended source and readout. Once delay-related residuals are included, the resulting budget can be tested against the assembled system and the recovery of a specified GW injection.
