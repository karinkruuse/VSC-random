\section{LISA Interferometric Detection System}
\label{sec:modelling}
We first write the LISA phase measurements and identify how laser and clock noise enter them. These expressions provide the reference for the laboratory readouts in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\subsection{Interferometric Phase Measurements}
Each LISA spacecraft carries two optical benches, with one laser on each bench. On a given bench, the local laser is compared with the laser on the other bench and with light received from a distant spacecraft. The science interferometer measures the inter-spacecraft phase difference; the test-mass and reference interferometers provide the local measurements needed to remove optical-bench motion. The upper panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:armlink} shows the science-interferometer part of a two-way link.

For the laser on \ac{MOSA} $ij$, on spacecraft $i$ facing spacecraft $j$, we write

\begin{equation}\label{total_phase}
    \Phi_{ij}(t) = \nu_{ij} t + \delta \phi_{ij}(t),
\end{equation}
where $\nu_{ij}$ denotes the nominal carrier frequency and $\delta \phi_{ij}(t)$ represents the intrinsic laser phase noise. Throughout this section, phases are expressed in cycles, frequencies in Hz, and clock errors in seconds. Capital $\Phi$ denotes total phase, while $\delta\phi$ denotes phase fluctuations about the nominal linear evolution. We use a single time coordinate, neglect relativistic time transformations, and retain constant nominal frequencies in the noise-coupling coefficients. The clock on spacecraft $i$ reads $t_i=t+q_i(t)$, where $q_i$ is its timing error.

An \ac{EOM} driven by the local USO adds phase-modulation sidebands to each laser field. Their modulation phase contains $\nu^m_{ij}q_i(t)$, so the transmitted light carries the local clock fluctuations. The clock error has one spacecraft index because both benches use the same USO.

The modulation tones are derived from the USO at approximately 2.4 GHz. A 1 MHz difference between the relevant tones separates the sideband--sideband beat notes from the carrier--carrier beat note. Excess noise in the tone-generation chain enters the clock-transfer measurement and is considered below.

The modulation depth $\mu_{\mathrm{EOM}}=0.53\,\mathrm{rad}$ places about $87\%$ of the optical power in the carrier and $6.5\%$ in each first-order sideband. The USO references the modulation, sampling-clock and pilot-tone synthesis chains; their role in clock-noise suppression is discussed in Section~\ref{clock noise}. Additional phase modulation with pseudo-random codes supports ranging and data transfer; it is distinct from the sinusoidal clock-transfer modulation considered here \cite{heinzel_auxiliary_2011}.

Propagation from \ac{MOSA} $ij$ to $ji$ delays the emitted phase and adds the GW response along the arm. Following the separation of background propagation and GW perturbations in \cite{bayle_unified_2023}, we write
\begin{equation}
    d^{\mathrm{tot}}_{ji}(t)=d_{ji}(t)+H_{ji}(t).
\end{equation}
Here $d_{ji}(t)$ is the background propagation time, which can vary slowly with the constellation breathing, and $H_{ji}(t)$ is its GW-induced perturbation. The first index denotes the receiving spacecraft and the second the emitting spacecraft.
We define the delay operator using only the background propagation time,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf D_{ji}x(t)=x\!\left(t-d_{ji}(t)\right).
\end{equation}
Applying the total propagation time to Eq.~\eqref{total_phase} and expanding to first order gives
\begin{align*}
    \Phi_{ij}\!\left(t-d_{ji}(t)-H_{ji}(t)\right)
    \simeq{}&\nu_{ij}\left[t-d_{ji}(t)\right]\\
    &+\mathbf D_{ji}\delta\phi_{ij}(t)
    -\nu_{ij}H_{ji}(t),
\end{align*}
where the product $H_{ji}\mathbf D_{ji}\delta\dot\phi_{ij}$ of the GW perturbation and laser-frequency noise has been neglected. We therefore introduce the GW phase contribution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gw_phase}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}(t)=-\nu_{ij}H_{ji}(t).
\end{equation}
To this order, the received total phase is $\mathbf D_{ji}\Phi_{ij}(t)+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}(t)$: the GW signal is an additive phase contribution, separate from the background delay operator. For constant $\nu_{ij}$, the corresponding frequency fluctuation is $\delta\dot\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}=-\nu_{ij}\dot H_{ji}$.

At spacecraft $j$, the received field $i$ interferes with the local laser field of phase $\Phi_{ji}(t)$ on a photodetector. The resulting heterodyne signal contains both the carrier–carrier beat note and the sideband–sideband beat notes. Additional cross terms lie outside the heterodyne range of the phasemeter; a representative LISA frequency plan uses absolute beat frequencies from 5 to 25 MHz \cite{heinzel_frequency_2024}.

The electrical signal from the photodetector is then digitized by the \ac{PMS} front-end electronics. The sampling process introduces a coupling of the local USO timing error $q_j(t)$ into the measured phase.
For all measurement readouts, we adopt the LISA convention that phases are written as ``distant minus local.''\footnote{The phasemeter measures the absolute beat frequency; its sign is assigned using the frequency plan.} We define the nominal carrier coefficient
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{ji}=\Delta\nu_{ji}=\nu_{ij}-\nu_{ji}.
\end{equation}
After removing the nominal beat evolution, the sampled carrier phase fluctuations are
\begin{equation}\label{Main_LISA_eq}
    \begin{aligned}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm c}_{ji}(t)={}&\mathbf D_{ji}\delta\phi_{ij}(t)-\delta\phi_{ji}(t)\\
    &+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}(t)-\alpha_{ji}q_j(t).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha_{ji}$ is the beat frequency in the fixed-frequency approximation used for the noise model. For an orbit-dependent frequency plan, it would be replaced by the actual signed beat frequency, including Doppler shifts.

The signal is then passed to \acp{DPLL}, which track the carrier and both sideband beat notes. In the following readouts, the nominal phase evolution is largely removed from the tracked phase, leaving the fluctuations used for GW detection and clock-noise suppression.
To connect this noise model to the measurements used for TDI, we also include the secondary disturbances in the science readout:
\begin{align}\label{eq:science_carrier}
    \text{sci}^{\text{c}}_{ji}(t) = & \mathbf{D}_{ji}\delta \phi_{ij}(t)-\delta \phi_{ji}(t)-\alpha_{ji}q_j(t)\nonumber\\[3pt]
    &+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}(t)+N^{\text{c}}_{ji}(t)\nonumber\\[3pt]
    &-\frac{\nu_0}{c}\left(\mathbf{n}_{ij}\cdot \mathbf{D}_{ji}\bm{\Delta}_{ij}(t)+\mathbf{n}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Delta}_{ji}(t)\right),
\end{align}
The term $N^{\mathrm{c}}_{ji}(t)$ contains readout noise from optical path-length fluctuations and electronics. The vectors $\bm{\Delta}_{ij}(t)$ are optical-bench displacements in metres. The unit vector $\mathbf n_{ji}$ points along the incoming beam from spacecraft $i$ to spacecraft $j$, and $\mathbf n_{ij}=-\mathbf n_{ji}$ in the geometry used for these displacement terms.
The factor $\nu_0/c=1/\lambda_0$ converts path-length fluctuations to phase in cycles, with $\nu_0\simeq281\,\mathrm{THz}$ for the nominal wavelength $\lambda_0=1064\,\mathrm{nm}$. MHz-scale offsets between lasers are small compared with this optical frequency, so a common $\nu_0$ suffices for the displacement terms. Those offsets remain essential in the heterodyne clock coefficients, which depend on frequency differences.

The corresponding upper sideband–sideband beat note readout can be written as
\begin{align}\label{SB_meas}
    \text{sci}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ji}(t)=&
    \mathbf{D}_{ji}\delta\phi_{ij}(t)- \delta\phi_{ji}(t)- \gamma_{ji}\, q_j(t)\nonumber\\[3pt]
    &+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}(t)+N^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ji}(t)\nonumber\\[3pt]
    &-\frac{\nu_0}{c}\left(\mathbf{n}_{ij}\cdot \mathbf{D}_{ji}\bm{\Delta}_{ij}(t)+\mathbf{n}_{ji}\cdot\bm{\Delta}_{ji}(t)\right)
    \nonumber\\[3pt]
    &+\nu^m_{ij} \mathbf{D}_{ji} q_i(t)- \nu^m_{ji} q_j(t).
\end{align}
The sideband clock coupling coefficient is
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_{ji} =\Delta\nu_{ji}+\nu^m_{ij}-\nu^m_{ji}=\Delta\nu_{ji}+\Delta\nu^m_{ji}= \Delta\nu_{ji}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}.
\end{equation}
Here $N^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ji}$ contains readout noise in the upper-sideband channel. The lower sideband has the same laser phase noise but the opposite sign for each modulation contribution.
The sidebands have optical frequencies $\nu_{ij}\pm\nu^m_{ij}$, so the same path-length change produces a slightly different phase shift in each sideband. Using $\nu_0/c$ for their displacement couplings neglects a fractional correction of order $\nu^m/\nu_0\simeq9\times10^{-6}$. Similarly, the upper-sideband GW phase is $-(\nu_{ij}+\nu^m_{ij})H_{ji}$, differing from the carrier by $-\nu^m_{ij}H_{ji}$. We neglect this small difference in the readout equations above and use the same $\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}$ for the carrier and both sidebands. The $\delta$ denotes a perturbation to the nominal optical phase, rather than stochastic noise.

The test-mass and reference measurements are, on \ac{MOSA} $ji$
\begin{align}
\text{tm}_{ji}(t)= &
\delta\phi_{jk}(t)-\delta\phi_{ji}(t)
+\beta_{ji}q_j(t)+\mu_{j}(t)+N^{\text{tm}}_{ji}(t)\nonumber\\[3pt]
&+\frac{2\nu_0}{c}
\mathbf{n}_{ji}\cdot\left(\bm{\delta}_{ji}(t)-\bm{\Delta}_{ji}(t)\right),\label{eq:testmass_readout}\\[5pt]
\text{ref}_{ji}(t)&=\delta\phi_{jk}(t)-\delta\phi_{ji}(t)+\beta_{ji}q_j(t)\nonumber\\
&\quad+\mu_{j}(t)+N^{\text{ref}}_{ji}(t).\label{eq:reference_readout}
\end{align}
The backlink term $\mu_j$ describes noise in the fibre connecting the two optical benches on spacecraft $j$. We assume reciprocal propagation through this fibre. The vector $\bm\delta_{ji}$ is the test-mass displacement, including motion produced by acceleration noise.

With the same sampling convention, the reference and test-mass clock coefficient is
\begin{equation}
    \beta_{ji}=\nu_{ji}-\nu_{jk}.
\end{equation}
It has the opposite sign to the adjacent-minus-local beat frequency, which explains the plus sign multiplying $\beta_{ji}q_j$ in the readouts.

These equations retain the terms needed to explain laser- and clock-noise suppression. A full instrument model includes further effects, such as tilt-to-length coupling and additional stray-light and optical-path disturbances. The miniLISA model omits the test-mass and reference interferometers and their associated bench-motion, backlink, and test-mass noise terms.


\subsection{Laser Noise and \ac{TDI}}
Pre-stabilisation reduces laser frequency noise before the measurements are combined. Offset phase locks keep the inter-spacecraft beat notes within the readout band, but the remaining laser noise still exceeds the required displacement sensitivity. TDI removes it by combining measurements in which the same laser fluctuation has propagated along different paths.

For scale, consider a representative pre-stabilized frequency-noise amplitude of $30\,\mathrm{Hz}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ \cite{bayle_unified_2023}. At Fourier frequency $f=10\,\mathrm{mHz}$, the corresponding single-laser phase-noise amplitude is $30/(2\pi f)\simeq480\,\mathrm{cycles}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$, equivalent to about $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ displacement noise at 1064 nm. This illustrative conversion is many orders of magnitude above picometre-scale displacement noise required for GW detection.

The construction proceeds in two steps: local measurements first remove optical-bench motion and reduce the six laser noises to three; delayed combinations of the resulting links then suppress the remaining laser noise \cite{otto2012tdi,tinto2018time}.
Using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:science_carrier}, \eqref{eq:testmass_readout}, and \eqref{eq:reference_readout}, we form
\begin{align*}
    T_{ji}&=\text{tm}_{ji}-\text{ref}_{ji},\\
    \xi_{ji}&=\text{sci}^{\mathrm c}_{ji}
    -\frac12\left(T_{ji}+\mathbf D_{ji}T_{ij}\right).
\end{align*}
Within the common-optical-frequency approximation above, the bench terms cancel, leaving the test-mass contribution
\begin{equation*}
    M_{ji}=-\frac{\nu_0}{c}\left[
    \mathbf n_{ji}\cdot\bm\delta_{ji}(t)
    +\mathbf D_{ji}\!\left(\mathbf n_{ij}\cdot\bm\delta_{ij}(t)\right)
    \right].
\end{equation*}
The following equations omit readout noises. Reciprocal backlink noise cancels in the local reference difference
\begin{equation*}
    z_{ji}=\frac12\left(\text{ref}_{ji}-\text{ref}_{jk}\right)
    =\delta\phi_{jk}-\delta\phi_{ji}+\beta_{ji}q_j.
\end{equation*}
For cyclic triples $(i,j,k)=(1,2,3),(2,3,1),(3,1,2)$, the intermediate variables can then be defined as
\begin{align*}
    \eta_{ji}&=\xi_{ji}-z_{ji}\\
    &=\mathbf D_{ji}\delta\phi_{ij}(t)-\delta\phi_{jk}(t)
    -(\alpha_{ji}+\beta_{ji})q_j(t)\\
    &\quad+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ji}(t)+M_{ji}(t),\\[3pt]
    \eta_{ij}&=\xi_{ij}+\mathbf D_{ij}z_{ji}\\
    &=\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_{jk}(t)-\delta\phi_{ij}(t)
    -\alpha_{ij}q_i(t)\\
    &\quad+\mathbf D_{ij}\!\left[\beta_{ji}q_j(t)\right]\\
    &\quad+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)+M_{ij}(t).
\end{align*}
Here $M_{ij}$ is obtained by exchanging $i$ and $j$ in $M_{ji}$. These combinations reduce the number of independent laser phase noises from six to three, retaining $\delta\phi_{12}$, $\delta\phi_{23}$ and $\delta\phi_{31}$, while removing the optical-bench displacement terms. The construction introduces an asymmetry between the two link orientations.

For a Michelson combination centred on spacecraft $i$, we first form the round-trip measurements
\begin{equation*}
    R_{ij}=\eta_{ij}+\mathcal D_{ij}\eta_{ji},\qquad
    R_{ik}=\eta_{ik}+\mathcal D_{ik}\eta_{ki}.
\end{equation*}
The first-generation combination is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:michelson_tdi}
    X_i=(1-\mathcal D_{ij}\mathcal D_{ji})R_{ik}
       -(1-\mathcal D_{ik}\mathcal D_{ki})R_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf D$ describes propagation in the measurement chain, while $\mathcal D$ denotes a delay applied in post-processing. Cancellation requires the processing delays to match the physical delays. A mismatch $\delta d$ leaves a phase residual proportional to $\delta d\,\delta\dot\phi$, so either an inaccurate delay estimate or an untracked change in the physical delay can limit laser-noise suppression.

First-generation \ac{TDI} combinations cancel laser noise for constant light-travel times. Orbital motion makes these delays time-dependent, so their order of application matters and the cancellation is no longer exact. Second-generation combinations use longer sequences of delays to cancel the leading residual from arm-length changes, leaving terms involving higher powers of the arm velocities and arm accelerations \cite{tinto2018time}.




\subsection{Clock-noise Coupling and Correction}
\label{clock noise}
Clock error enters a carrier phase measurement multiplied by the local beat frequency. The optical sidebands provide an additional measurement of the transmitted clock phase, allowing the remote and local clocks to be compared \cite{hartwig_clock-jitter_2021}. Subtracting the carrier measurement from the upper-sideband measurement removes their common laser phase. Neglecting readout and modulation noise for the moment, we obtain

\begin{equation}\label{correction variables}
    r_{ji} = \frac{\text{sci}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ji}(t)- \text{sci}^{\mathrm{c}}_{ji}(t)}{\nu^m_{ij}}=\mathbf{D}_{ji}q_i(t)-q_j(t).
\end{equation}
The retained GW phase cancels in this difference because we have approximated the carrier and sideband GW couplings as equal. Keeping their optical-frequency difference would instead add $-H_{ji}$ to $r_{ji}$; the cancellation is therefore approximate. The normalization uses the transmitted modulation frequency $\nu^m_{ij}$, giving $r_{ji}$ units of seconds. Equivalently, $r_{ji}=(\text{sci}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ji}-\text{sci}^{\mathrm{sb,-}}_{ji})/(2\nu^m_{ij})$ under the same approximations. These variables contain delayed remote clock noise minus local clock noise, matching the structure of laser noise in the science readouts. Delayed combinations of the $r$ variables can therefore be used to construct clock corrections for the TDI observables \cite{hartwig_clock-jitter_2021}.
The modulation tone must carry the same timing fluctuations that enter the phase measurement. In LISA, pilot-tone correction removes the ADC sampling jitter, making the pilot tone the relevant clock reference. Noise added between this reference and the modulation tone limits the subsequent clock correction. Let $\tau_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)$ denote differential timing noise in the tone derivation for MOSA $ij$, in seconds, and let $\psi_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)$ denote additional optical modulation phase noise, in cycles. The excess upper-sideband phase is
\begin{equation*}
    m_{ij}(t)=\nu^m_{ij}\tau_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)+\psi_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}(t).
\end{equation*}
Including these terms and readout noise gives
\begin{align*}
    r_{ji}={}&\mathbf D_{ji}q_i-q_j
    +\frac{\mathbf D_{ji}m_{ij}-m_{ji}}{\nu^m_{ij}}\\
    &+\frac{N^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ji}-N^{\mathrm c}_{ji}}{\nu^m_{ij}}.
\end{align*}
Modulation and readout noise are kept separate because their transfer functions differ. A source modulation error appears in both local and delayed measurements, while a readout error is added in a particular measurement channel. Independent sources may be uncorrelated, but repeated appearances of the same source must still be combined before calculating its output noise power.

Dividing the modulation phase by its frequency gives $m_{ij}/\nu^m_{ij}=\tau_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}+\psi_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}/\nu^m_{ij}$. A higher modulation frequency therefore reduces the effect of a fixed phase noise, but leaves a fixed timing noise unchanged. Improving that timing-noise floor requires a quieter tone-generation chain. When the local and transmitted frequencies differ, the local timing contribution retains the factor $\nu^m_{ji}/\nu^m_{ij}$. A measurement at one modulation frequency cannot distinguish the two contributions.

The two modulation-frequency synthesis chains can add different excess noises. Local reference-interferometer sideband measurements provide additional observables for correcting the accessible differential part \cite{hartwig_clock-jitter_2021}. This contribution is absent from the present one-modulator-per-spacecraft testbed.
