\section{miniLISA Design}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Motivation for the Present Work}
The present work extends the electronic delay-line testbed with optical inputs and independent spacecraft clocks. It combines physical laser noise, sideband-based clock transfer, and hardware delays of several seconds, and uses component measurements to estimate the performance of the combined setup.

The delays allow us to study how light-travel times interact with clock synchronization and digital signal processing. Time-varying delays are particularly relevant: their order matters in TDI, and applying a delay before filtering need not give the same result as applying it afterwards. The hardware implementation makes these effects accessible to experimental tests.

\subsection{Measurement Philosophy}
The testbed reproduces the laser-noise, clock-noise, and GW couplings of the LISA measurements. The first index of a link denotes the receiving \ac{SC}; source quantities carry a single spacecraft index, $\delta\phi_i$, $q_i$, and $\nu_i^m$. The target carrier phase fluctuations are
\begin{equation*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm c}_{ij}(t)
    =\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j(t)-\delta\phi_i(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)-\alpha_{ij}q_i(t),
\end{equation*}
and the upper-sideband fluctuations are
\begin{align*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ij}(t)={}&
    \mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j(t)-\delta\phi_i(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)\\
    &-(\alpha_{ij}+\nu_j^m)q_i(t)+\nu_j^m\mathbf D_{ij}q_j(t).
\end{align*}
Here $\alpha_{ij}$ is the signed nominal carrier beat frequency. The sideband sampling coefficient is $\gamma_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}+\nu_j^m-\nu_i^m$; combining it with the local modulation phase gives the clock term above. Both channels use the common GW phase approximation of Section~\ref{sec:modelling}. We call a measurement \emph{LISA-like} when it reproduces these couplings. Modulation, board, and readout noises are added below.

An FPGA delay line supplies the propagation delay and the prescribed Doppler and GW frequency shifts. Heterodyning the optical fields with a common reference laser brings their phase information into the MHz range accessible to the electronics. The reference laser noise becomes part of the effective input laser noise, whose cancellation is discussed below.

The testbed omits the test-mass and reference interferometers, together with test-mass acceleration noise, optical-bench motion, backlink noise, and tilt-to-length coupling. Consequently, the local combinations $T_{ji}$ and $\xi_{ji}$ used to remove bench motion in Section~\ref{sec:modelling} are unnecessary. Identifying the two bench lasers on each spacecraft with a single source also removes the need for the reference differences $z_{ji}$: the synthesized links directly reproduce the three-laser structure of the $\eta$ observables.

\subsection{Optical Input}
Three lasers represent the spacecraft, each with an independent clock driving its modulation and phase measurement. A fourth laser provides the common optical reference. The setup supports frequency-offset locking between the spacecraft lasers and modulation for clock-tone transfer.
The role of the reference laser is to convert the optical phase fluctuations of each \ac{SC} laser into electronically processable heterodyne beat notes, which can then be measured by the delay line. This heterodyning step effectively downconverts the optical signals from approximately 281 THz to tens of megahertz, preserving laser frequency noise in the millihertz range of interest.
Among the \ac{SC} lasers, one is frequency-stabilized to a 25 cm \ac{ULE} glass cavity, while the reference laser is locked to a 30 cm \ac{ULE} cavity via the Pound-Drever-Hall method.
Both cavities are housed in a vacuum chamber surrounded by three layers of aluminum thermal shielding. Based on simulations and experience with cavity stabilization, the target is a pre-stabilized \ac{LFN} level of around 30 Hz/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ in the millihertz band; this level has not yet been demonstrated in the present setup. The remaining two \ac{SC} lasers are PLL-locked to the synthetic LISA-like beat notes.

Each \ac{SC} laser is phase-modulated using a fiber-coupled broadband EOM driven at a frequency derived from the same independent USO that clocks the corresponding phasemeter ADC.
The reference laser is unmodulated in the configuration described here, so the spacecraft carrier and sidebands each beat with the reference carrier. The modulation frequency must therefore keep all three electrical beat notes within the delay-line input bandwidth. These input tones carry the optical phase and clock information; the LISA-like carrier--carrier and sideband--sideband readouts are formed by digital subtraction downstream. Modulating the reference laser is a possible extension for higher modulation frequencies, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:clock_extensions}.
The construction of a two-way arm link is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:armlink}(b), alongside a simplified schematic of the corresponding LISA link showing only the functionally relevant elements. Taking the signed photodetector beat phase as \ac{SC} laser minus reference laser, the carrier phase can be written as
\begin{align*}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{PD},i}^{\mathrm c}(t)
    ={}&\nu_{\mathrm{R},i}t
    +\delta\phi_i(t)-\delta\phi_{\mathrm{R}}(t),
\end{align*}
where $\nu_{\mathrm{R},i}$ is the signed nominal beat note frequency between the reference laser and the \ac{SC} $i$ laser.
The upper-sideband phase is
\begin{align*}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{PD},i}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}(t)
    ={}&\left(\nu_{\mathrm{R},i}+\nu_i^m\right)t\\
    &+\delta\phi_i(t)-\delta\phi_{\mathrm{R}}(t)
    +\nu_i^m q_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_i^m N_i(t)+N_i^m(t).
\end{align*}
Here, $N_i$ is the differential timing noise between the modulation and the effective \ac{SC} measurement-clock reference, while $N_i^m$ is the additional modulation phase noise.
These are the single-index counterparts of $N_{ij}$ and $N^m_{ij}$ in Section~\ref{clock noise}. We retain both contributions and write their combined upper-sideband phase noise as
\begin{equation*}
    m_i(t)=\nu_i^m N_i(t)+N_i^m(t).
\end{equation*}
This noise is added to the modulation before the signal is split between the boards. It therefore follows the \ac{SC} sideband through the local and delayed paths. The lower sideband carries the opposite sign of the modulation phase, including $\nu_i^m q_i$ and $m_i$.

Our modulation-noise measurement constrains the combined phase noise $m_i$ at the measurement frequency; it does not distinguish $N_i$ from $N_i^m$. The distinction matters when predicting the effect of increasing the modulation frequency. With respect to timing, the excess is
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{m_i}{\nu_i^m}=N_i+\frac{N_i^m}{\nu_i^m}.
\end{equation*}

Photodetection and its electronics introduce additive noise from relative intensity fluctuations, shot noise and detector electronics. This contributes to the phase estimated by the delay-line PLLs. For the performance estimate, we treat the resulting readout contributions as uncorrelated between measurement paths, without taking credit for possible cancellation of shared noise by TDI. Their equivalent phase noise is included together with the uncorrelated electronic noise in the final readout terms below.

The photodetector output is then split and distributed across three delay-line boards. One of these boards synthesizes the local LISA-like beat note signals, the other two do so for the other two \ac{SC}. The signal routing between the boards is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:triangular}.



\subsection{The Delay Line}
\label{sec:delay_line}
The digital delay line emulates the light-travel time along each directed link. The implemented memory configuration supports delays up to 16 s, covering LISA's nominal one-way light-travel time of about 8.3 s. The available delay range depends on the memory allocation and the rate at which tracked frequency records are stored.

The implementation uses an AMD Xilinx ZCU208 RFSoC evaluation board, with integrated ADCs and DACs, FPGA programmable logic, and an embedded \ac{PS}. For each mixed link output, two ADC paths supply the remote and local electrical signals. Separate DPLLs track their carriers and sidebands. The remote frequency records are filtered, decimated, and stored in DDR4 memory; the local records supply the undelayed input to the digital subtraction. The converters run at 2 GHz, the FPGA signal path at 125 MHz, and the remote records are stored at 15.625 MHz.

Orbital delays and GW link responses are computed in advance in Python and uploaded to the \ac{PS}. During playback, the \ac{PS} converts the model inputs into fixed-point commands and streams timestamped records to the programmable logic. The initial delay sets the memory read--write separation. Subsequent delay-rate commands control traversal through the stored records, with linear interpolation between adjacent records providing the fractional delay. The FPGA applies each command at its scheduled clock cycle; software supplies the stream without determining the precise actuation time. The interpolation grid alone does not establish the achieved delay accuracy, which also depends on filtering, signal bandwidth, and numerical precision.

After delayed readout, the FPGA adds the prescribed Doppler and GW frequency offsets and subtracts the tracked local frequency. It accumulates the resulting frequency separately for the carrier and both sidebands, synthesizes the three sinusoids, and combines them for the mixed DAC output. Doppler offsets evolve by accumulating the streamed Doppler-rate commands, whereas the GW command is added directly as a frequency offset. Section~\ref{sec:gw_injection} describes its optical scaling. Omitting the nominal frequency evolution, the intended carrier phase is
\begin{gather*}
    \mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j^{\mathrm{eff}}
    -\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij},\\
    \delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}=\delta\phi_i-\delta\phi_{\mathrm R}.
\end{gather*}

The two ADC inputs and the DAC output share the timing jitter $\epsilon_i(t)$ of the board on spacecraft $i$. Sampling imprints this jitter on both inputs. The remote contribution then passes through the delay, while subtracting the local signal in the digital mixer reverses the sign of its ADC contribution. The DAC adds jitter at the output beat frequency $\alpha_{ij}$, giving
\begin{align*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm c}_{\epsilon,ij}(t)={}&
    -\nu_{\mathrm R,j}\mathbf D_{ij}\epsilon_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_{\mathrm R,i}\epsilon_i(t)+\alpha_{ij}\epsilon_i(t)\\
    ={}&\nu_{\mathrm R,j}(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t).
\end{align*}
Here $\alpha_{ij}=\nu_{\mathrm R,j}-\nu_{\mathrm R,i}$, so the local ADC and DAC terms combine to give $\nu_{\mathrm R,j}\epsilon_i(t)$. The residual is therefore proportional to the difference between the board jitter now and one light-travel time earlier.

The same cancellation applies to the upper sideband, with the remote input frequency replaced by $\nu_{\mathrm R,j}+\nu_j^m$:
\begin{equation*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{\epsilon,ij}(t)
    =(\nu_{\mathrm R,j}+\nu_j^m)(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t).
\end{equation*}
Both expressions assume constant nominal frequencies and no additional output frequency offset.

We group the remaining uncorrelated board noise with optical and phasemeter readout noise in $b^a_{ij}$ below. Modulation noise is kept separate because it follows the source through both local and delayed paths. When using the measured electronic baseline, contributions already present in that measurement are counted only once.

\subsection{GW Signal Injection}
\label{sec:gw_injection}
The GW phase defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gw_phase} is implemented electronically after the background delay. The required phase on the link from $j$ to $i$ is
\begin{equation*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)=-\nu_jH_{ij}(t).
\end{equation*}
Since the nominal optical frequency $\nu_j$ is constant, the frequency offset supplied to the phase accumulator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gw_frequency_command}
    \delta\nu^{\mathrm{inj}}_{ij}(t)
    =\delta\dot\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)
    =-\nu_j\dot H_{ij}(t).
\end{equation}
Accumulating this offset reproduces the required GW phase up to an initial phase constant. The scale factor is the represented optical frequency, not the MHz beat note frequency entering the delay-line ADC. Thus the GW signal is generated by adding phase evolution to the reconstructed signal; the hardware delay itself remains $d_{ij}$ and is not additionally perturbed by $H_{ij}$. This implements the first-order propagation model of Section~\ref{sec:modelling} without requiring the delay register to resolve the GW-induced change in light-travel time.

The waveform supplied for each directed link is evaluated at its reception time in the common modelling time coordinate. The propagation and source geometry are already included in $H_{ij}$, so no additional background delay is applied to the injection waveform after it is generated. Relative timing between the playback streams must reproduce this convention.

The same frequency offset is added to the carrier and both sideband phase accumulators in the present implementation. This realizes the common-GW-phase approximation used in Section~\ref{sec:modelling}. Resolving the relative sideband correction would instead require offsets proportional to $\nu_j\pm\nu_j^m$, but that correction is neglected here. Consequently, the prescribed GW phase cancels from the sideband-minus-carrier clock variable, while remaining in the individual science readouts and their TDI combinations.

When the link response is supplied as a fractional-frequency signal $y_{ij}=-\dot H_{ij}$, the command is $\delta\nu^{\mathrm{inj}}_{ij}=\nu_jy_{ij}$, using $\nu_j\simeq c/(1064\,\mathrm{nm})$ for the optical scale.
The link responses can be computed in Python for a chosen waveform and constellation geometry. Multiple sources can be combined by summing their link responses in the linear regime. The source class is not fixed by the hardware, but the resulting commands must lie within its frequency range, resolution and update bandwidth. A set of independently prescribed link signals can also be used for calibration; a GW realization requires the relative amplitudes and phases appropriate to a common source and geometry.

In the delay-line implementation, a signed 32-bit GW word $K_{ij}$ is added on the 64-bit phase-increment scale, giving
\begin{equation*}
    \delta\nu^{\mathrm{inj}}_{ij}=K_{ij}\frac{f_{\mathrm{clk}}}{2^{64}}.
\end{equation*}
For the nominal 125 MHz accumulator clock, this corresponds to approximately $6.78\times10^{-12}$ Hz per least significant bit and an offset range of $\pm0.01455$ Hz. The word is sign-extended to 64 bits without changing its frequency scale and held between timestamped updates. The realized phase accuracy depends on command quantization, update timing, and output phase reconstruction, so command resolution alone does not establish injection fidelity.

\subsection{Readout and Data Processing}

The electrical signals produced by the delay line are passed to a phasemeter for digitization and phase extraction. In the current implementation, we use a Moku:Pro phasemeter. In future iterations, this will be replaced by a ZCU104 evaluation board with custom front-end electronics, allowing closer replication of LISA phasemeter features such as pilot-tone correction and digital filtering.

With the local-interferometer simplifications described above, the synthesized links can be used directly as $\eta$ observables. Retaining the modulation, board, and readout noises, the carrier measurement is
\begin{align*}
    \eta_{ij}(t)
    ={}&\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)-\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)
    -\alpha_{ij}q_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_{\mathrm R,j}(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t)
    +b_{ij}^{\mathrm c}(t),
\end{align*}
and the corresponding upper-sideband--upper-sideband beat note as
\begin{align*}
    \eta_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}(t)
    ={}&\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)-\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)
    -\gamma_{ij}q_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_j^m\mathbf D_{ij}q_j(t)-\nu_i^m q_i(t)\\
    &+\mathbf D_{ij}\!\left[\nu_j^m N_j(t)+N_j^m(t)\right]\\
    &-\nu_i^m N_i(t)-N_i^m(t)\\
    &+(\nu_{\mathrm R,j}+\nu_j^m)(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t)
    +b_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}(t).
\end{align*}
The terms proportional to $q_i$ come from the final phasemeter clock, while those proportional to $\epsilon_i$ come from the delay-line board. These clocks can share a reference, so their timing errors may be correlated.

The additive phase noise $b^a_{ij}$ combines optical readout, residual board, and final-phasemeter noise, in cycles. Timing and modulation noise are already included explicitly. For the performance estimate, we treat $b^a_{ij}$ as independent between links and between carrier and sideband channels, but retain the same noise realization whenever a readout is reused in TDI or clock correction.

The reference laser phase noise enters through
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j^{\mathrm{eff}}-\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}
    =\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j-\delta\phi_i
    -(\mathbf D_{ij}-1)\delta\phi_{\mathrm{R}}.
\end{equation*}
It therefore has the same delayed-difference structure as the effective \ac{SC} laser noises and is treated by TDI in the same manner. The reference laser also introduces correlations between those effective noises. A quieter reference reduces its contribution, but independence of the effective laser noises is not required for the algebraic cancellation. Optical readout noises and phase-locking correlations are accounted for separately in the noise budget.

Finally, the upper-sideband-minus-carrier variable takes the form
\begin{align*}
    r_{ij}
    ={}&\frac{\eta_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}-\eta_{ij}}{\nu_j^m}\\
    ={}&\mathbf D_{ij}q_j-q_i
    +\frac{\mathbf D_{ij}m_j-m_i}{\nu_j^m}\\
    &+(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i
    +\frac{b_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}-b_{ij}^{\mathrm c}}{\nu_j^m}.
\end{align*}
The common GW phase cancels. Subtracting the carrier from the sideband also leaves a board-jitter coefficient of $\nu_j^m$, so division by $\nu_j^m$ gives $(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i$. The modulation contribution is
\begin{align*}
    \frac{\mathbf D_{ij}m_j-m_i}{\nu_j^m}
    ={}&\mathbf D_{ij}N_j-\frac{\nu_i^m}{\nu_j^m}N_i\\
    &+\frac{\mathbf D_{ij}N_j^m-N_i^m}{\nu_j^m}.
\end{align*}
Increasing both modulation frequencies at fixed ratio suppresses the last line for fixed direct phase noises, but does not suppress the timing-jitter terms in the first line. Since the present modulation measurement does not separate these contributions, both possibilities must be retained when projecting the performance of a higher-frequency implementation. Their propagation through TDI and clock correction is considered in the performance section.

Backlink noise, arising from the fiber connecting the two optical benches on a given spacecraft, is similarly absent from the current configuration.
\subsection{Future Extensions: Injection of Acceleration Noise}
\subsection{Future Extensions: Three Satellite Constellation}
A natural extension of the current setup is the inclusion of a third inter-spacecraft link. Once the two-arm configuration has been validated, adding a third arm would enable tests of a broader class of \ac{TDI} combinations and provide closer correspondence to the full LISA geometry. As the present testbed is constrained to one laser per spacecraft, extending the setup to three arms requires careful frequency planning to ensure that all heterodyne beatnotes remain within the bandwidth of the phasemeter.

For tests of first-generation \ac{TDI} with static delays, keeping the three arising beatnote frequencies within the LISA phasemeter range is expected to be straightforward. When considering second-generation \ac{TDI} with Doppler shifts, however, maintaining all beatnotes within the nominal 7–23 MHz range may become challenging. For experimental \ac{TDI} validation, one could increase the phasemeter bandwidth. Alternatively, additional frequency shifters could be introduced to provide greater flexibility in controlling the beatnote frequencies.

\subsection{Future Extensions: Changes to the Clock Noise Transfer System}
\label{sec:clock_extensions}
Currently, clock-noise removal performance is ultimately limited by modulation noise arising from the generation of the clock sidebands. In LISA, this limitation is associated with the GHz frequency conversion processes and the subsequent modulation. In the current testbed, this effect is expected to be less pronounced, as the spacecraft laser fields are modulated at MHz frequencies, simplifying the sideband generation chain.

A future upgrade would replace the MHz modulation with GHz modulation to more closely reflect LISA. This requires downconversion of the sidebands into the delay-line input band, which spans 10--60 MHz in the configuration described in Section~\ref{sec:delay_line}. Modulating the reference laser as well would allow corresponding optical sidebands to beat within that band.

Another possible change is the inclusion of at least one more modulator for one or all spacecraft and the corresponding reference measurement. For LISA, the modulation frequency conversion for the right side \ac{MOSA} is known to be noisier compared to the left side, as the conversion multiplication implemented digitally does not correspond to a power of two. Hence, in the clock noise removal process, the correcting variables as defined by equation (\ref{correction variables}) have to be corrected themselves using the purely local sideband-sideband measurements. To include this effect, our testbed would need at least one spacecraft with explicit left and right side separation. This could be realized by introducing a second modulator driven by a deliberately noisier signal.

\section{Conclusion}\label{Conclusion}
We have presented the design and current status of miniLISA, a testbed extending prior FPGA-based TDI demonstrations to include physically representative optical laser noise and independently running \ac{SC} clocks within a single hardware framework. Unlike previous testbeds, which have each reproduced a subset of these elements — physical lasers without independent clocks or realistic delays (UFLIS), independent clocks without programmable delays (Hexagon), or realistic delays without physical optical generation (LISA on Table) — miniLISA combines real lasers, independent Rb-disciplined clocks, second-scale programmable delays, and a three-\ac{SC} topology in one setup.

The current noise budget shows a mixed picture. Readout noise — relative intensity noise and shot noise on the photodetectors — is analytically predicted to remain below the 1 pm displacement allocation across the core LISA band, suggesting it will not be the dominant limitation, though this remains a provisional conclusion pending full system integration. Delay-board clock jitter and the residual clock-noise coupling, by contrast, currently exceed the allocation by several orders of magnitude in both the analytical model and initial measurements. We have identified the dominant mechanism — board clock noise entering through the ADC/DAC chain, uncorrected by TDI or the standard clock-noise-removal algorithm — and outlined a correction scheme based on cross-board reference measurements, along with two independent mitigations for the underlying modulation-noise limitation: balanced detection, and an upgrade to GHz-frequency sideband modulation that would restore LISA-representative signal-to-readout-noise ratios in the clock-correcting variables.

Several open questions remain under active investigation, including the extent to which the current single-board measurements are limited by ranging error rather than by the mechanisms modeled here, and the characterization of second-order couplings not yet captured in the present noise model. Planned extensions include the addition of a third inter-spacecraft arm to enable genuine three-signal TDI combinations rather than a two-way approximation, and the GHz modulation upgrade described above. Together, these developments aim to bring miniLISA's intrinsic noise floor below LISA-representative levels across all reproduced channels, at which point the transfer-function and suppression-ratio comparisons presented here can serve as a genuine, physically-generated test of the LISA measurement model rather than a characterization of the testbed's own limitations.
