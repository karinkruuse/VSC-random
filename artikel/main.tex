\begin{abstract}
miniLISA is a laboratory testbed for testing laser- and clock-noise suppression together with gravitational-wave signal recovery. Optical inputs provide laser phase noise and modulation sidebands, while FPGA delay lines represent inter-spacecraft propagation and add prescribed signal responses. We relate the synthesized readouts to a reduced LISA measurement model and propagate measured electronic and modulation spectra through second-generation time-delay interferometry with clock correction. An order-of-magnitude detector calculation lies below these contributions for the assumed optical powers. The electronic baseline is the larger measured contribution over most of the plotted band. The common board-reference fluctuation is suppressed by TDI and clock correction; differential board timing is assumed negligible in the component sum. This assumption and the remaining delay errors require experimental verification.

\end{abstract}

\section{miniLISA Design}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Experimental Concept}
The optical extension combines physical laser noise, sideband clock transfer and electronic delays of several seconds. The delays place samples acquired at different times in the same measurement, as inter-spacecraft propagation does in LISA. The following model specifies the couplings that the hardware must reproduce.

\subsection{Synthesized LISA Measurements}
The design has six directed links between three spacecraft. Each spacecraft is represented by one effective laser phase $\delta\phi_i$ and one clock timing error $q_i$. With the first link index denoting the receiver, the target carrier measurement is

\begin{equation*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm c}_{ij}(t)
    =\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j(t)-\delta\phi_i(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)-\alpha_{ij}q_i(t),
\end{equation*}
and the upper-sideband fluctuations are
\begin{align*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{ij}(t)={}&
    \mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j(t)-\delta\phi_i(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)\\
    &-(\alpha_{ij}+\nu_j^m)q_i(t)+\nu_j^m\mathbf D_{ij}q_j(t).
\end{align*}
Here $\alpha_{ij}$ is the signed nominal carrier beat frequency and $\nu_i^m$ is the modulation frequency on spacecraft $i$. The sideband sampling coefficient is $\gamma_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}+\nu_j^m-\nu_i^m$; combining it with the local modulation phase gives the clock term above. Both channels use the common GW phase approximation of Section~\ref{sec:modelling}. We call a measurement \emph{LISA-like} when it reproduces these couplings. Modulation, board, and readout noises are added below.

The electronic delays reproduce propagation between spacecraft and add prescribed Doppler and GW frequency shifts. Heterodyning against a common reference laser brings the optical signals into the MHz input band. The reference phase becomes part of each effective laser phase and enters the same TDI cancellation.

Unlike LISA, miniLISA uses one laser per spacecraft and has no test-mass or reference interferometers. Bench-motion corrections $T_{ji}$ and $\xi_{ji}$ and reference differences $z_{ji}$ are therefore unnecessary. The synthesized links reproduce the three-laser $\eta$ measurements, with the reference-interferometer clock terms proportional to $\beta_{ji}$ absent. Test-mass acceleration, bench motion, backlink noise and tilt-to-length coupling are omitted.

\subsection{Optical Input}
Three lasers represent the spacecraft, and a fourth laser supplies their common optical reference. Heterodyning each spacecraft laser with the reference produces a MHz beat note that retains their relative phase fluctuations. These beat notes form the inputs to the electronic delay lines.

One spacecraft laser is stabilised to a 25 cm \ac{ULE} cavity; the reference laser is locked to a separate 30 cm cavity by the Pound--Drever--Hall method. Both cavities are housed in a vacuum chamber with three layers of aluminium thermal shielding. The target laser frequency noise is approximately 30 Hz/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ in the millihertz band. The other two spacecraft lasers are phase-locked to synthesized beat notes.

Each spacecraft uses an independent timing reference for its Moku. The Moku supplies both the phase readout and the modulation tone driving the fiber-coupled EOM. We use $q_i$ for the timing fluctuations common to the EOM drive and the phasemeter's sampling reference. Excess noise between these paths enters the modulation and readout terms separately.

The reference laser is unmodulated, so its carrier beats with the spacecraft carrier and both sidebands. The optical frequency plan must keep these three input tones within the delay-line bandwidth. Digital subtraction then forms the carrier--carrier and sideband--sideband measurements of the target model. A possible extension with a modulated reference is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:clock_extensions}.

The lower panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:armlink} shows one two-way laboratory link. Taking the photodetector beat phase as spacecraft laser minus reference laser, the carrier phase before detector noise is

\begin{align*}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{PD},i}^{\mathrm c}(t)
    ={}&\nu_{\mathrm{R},i}t
    +\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(t),
\end{align*}
where $\nu_{\mathrm{R},i}$ is the signed nominal beat note frequency between the reference laser and the \ac{SC} $i$ laser, and $\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}=\delta\phi_i-\delta\phi_{\mathrm R}$ includes their relative laser phase noise.
The upper-sideband phase is
\begin{align*}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{PD},i}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}(t)
    ={}&\left(\nu_{\mathrm{R},i}+\nu_i^m\right)t\\
    &+\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    +\nu_i^m q_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_i^m \tau_i^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)+\psi_i^{\mathrm{mod}}(t).
\end{align*}
Here, $\tau_i^{\mathrm{mod}}$ is timing noise added by the modulation path relative to the common reference $q_i$, while $\psi_i^{\mathrm{mod}}$ is additional modulation phase noise. Noise added only by the sampling path is included in the readout terms below.
These are the single-index counterparts of $\tau_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}$ and $\psi_{ij}^{\mathrm{mod}}$ in Section~\ref{clock noise}, with the same dependence on modulation frequency. We write their combined upper-sideband phase noise as
\begin{equation*}
    m_i(t)=\nu_i^m \tau_i^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)+\psi_i^{\mathrm{mod}}(t).
\end{equation*}
The lower sideband carries the opposite modulation phase, $-\nu_i^m q_i-m_i$. If its signed electrical frequency $\nu_{\mathrm R,i}-\nu_i^m$ is negative, representing it at positive frequency also reverses the measured phase sign; the clock correction below uses the upper sideband.

Each photodetector output is split between the three delay boards. A board uses the local and remote inputs to synthesize the links received at its spacecraft. Figure~\ref{fig:triangular} shows the six-link routing.

Photocurrent noise produces phase errors when the delay-line PLLs track the input beats. Copies of a detector signal can share this error if the tracking responses match; ADC and electronics noise added after the split also contributes to each path. Section~\ref{sec:readout_noise} estimates the detector scale, and Section~\ref{sec:noise_propagation} describes the propagation of shared and independent sources.

\subsection{The Delay Line}
\label{sec:delay_line}
The digital delay line emulates the light-travel time along each directed link. The current firmware always tracks and stores the carrier and both sidebands. At the resulting data rate, the available memory supports a maximum delay of approximately 8.5 s, covering LISA's nominal one-way light-travel time of about 8.3 s.

The delay line uses an AMD Xilinx ZCU208 RFSoC board with integrated ADCs, DACs, FPGA logic and an embedded \ac{PS}. For each link, separate DPLLs track the carrier and sidebands in the remote and local inputs. The remote frequency records pass through a buffer before subtraction of the current local frequencies. The 125 MHz processing rate limits the input tones to below 62.5 MHz; the digitally synthesized output frequencies are set separately.

Orbital delays and GW link responses are computed in advance in Python and uploaded to the \ac{PS}. During playback, the \ac{PS} converts the model inputs into fixed-point commands and streams timestamped records to the programmable logic.
The buffer supplies the macroscopic light-travel time, while fine delay actuation follows the prescribed arm-length evolution using fractional-delay interpolation. The underlying electronic architecture is described in \cite{ferguson2025applying}.

The FPGA subtracts the local frequency from the delayed remote frequency and adds the prescribed Doppler and GW offsets. Section~\ref{sec:gw_injection} describes the GW scaling. Omitting the nominal frequency evolution, the intended carrier phase is
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j^{\mathrm{eff}}
    -\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}.
\end{equation*}
Section~\ref{sec:noise_propagation} propagates the component noises through ideal phase delays with changing arm lengths.

Board $i$ receives the detector signals representing spacecraft $i$ and $j$. It delays the $j$ signal and subtracts the current $i$ signal. The ADCs sampling these inputs and the DAC producing the synthesized beat share the board timing fluctuation $\epsilon_i$. Their carrier-phase contributions are

\begin{align*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm c}_{\epsilon,ij}(t)={}&
    -\nu_{\mathrm R,j}\mathbf D_{ij}\epsilon_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_{\mathrm R,i}\epsilon_i(t)+\alpha_{ij}\epsilon_i(t)\\
    ={}&\nu_{\mathrm R,j}(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t).
\end{align*}
The three terms are from sampling the stored $j$ input, sampling the current $i$ input, and generating the output, respectively. Using $\alpha_{ij}=\nu_{\mathrm R,j}-\nu_{\mathrm R,i}$ gives the final delayed difference.

The same cancellation applies to the upper sideband, with the remote input frequency replaced by $\nu_{\mathrm R,j}+\nu_j^m$:
\begin{equation*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm{sb,+}}_{\epsilon,ij}(t)
    =(\nu_{\mathrm R,j}+\nu_j^m)(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t).
\end{equation*}
Both expressions assume constant nominal frequencies and no additional output frequency offset.

Board timing also affects the duration of the stored delay. Let $t_{\mathrm{in}}$ be the physical time when the sample emerging at time $t$ entered the buffer. A delay command $d_{ij}$ is counted in board time $u_i(t)=t+\epsilon_i(t)$, so $u_i(t)-u_i(t_{\mathrm{in}})=d_{ij}$. To first order,
\begin{align}
    d^{\mathrm{phys}}_{ij}(t)&=t-t_{\mathrm{in}}
      =d_{ij}+\delta d^\epsilon_{ij}(t),\\
    \delta d^\epsilon_{ij}(t)&=-(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t).
    \label{eq:board_delay_jitter}
\end{align}
This matters when the delay used in TDI differs from the actual buffer delay: the laser phases are then combined at the wrong times and do not fully cancel. The component budget assumes matched delays and excludes this residual.

\subsection{GW Signal Injection}
\label{sec:gw_injection}
The GW phase defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gw_phase} is implemented electronically after the background delay. The required phase on the link from $j$ to $i$ is
\begin{equation*}
    \delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)=-\nu_jH_{ij}(t).
\end{equation*}
Since the nominal optical frequency $\nu_j$ is constant, the frequency offset supplied to the phase accumulator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gw_frequency_command}
    \delta\nu^{\mathrm{inj}}_{ij}(t)
    =\delta\dot\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)
    =-\nu_j\dot H_{ij}(t).
\end{equation}
Integrating this command gives the required GW phase, up to an initial constant. The conversion uses the represented optical frequency, rather than the MHz input beat frequency. The signal is added to the reconstructed phase after the background delay; the buffer delay itself is not changed by $H_{ij}$.

Each directed-link waveform is evaluated at its reception time $t$. The propagation and source geometry are already included in $H_{ij}$, so no additional background delay is applied to the injection waveform after it is generated. Relative timing between the playback streams must reproduce this convention.

The same GW offset is applied to the carrier and both sidebands, following the approximation in Section~\ref{sec:modelling}. It therefore cancels from their difference used for clock correction while remaining in the science signal.

When the link response is supplied as a fractional-frequency signal $y_{ij}=-\dot H_{ij}$, the command is $\delta\nu^{\mathrm{inj}}_{ij}=\nu_jy_{ij}$, using $\nu_j\simeq c/(1064\,\mathrm{nm})$ for the optical scale.
The link responses can be computed in Python for a chosen waveform and constellation geometry. Multiple sources can be combined by summing their link responses in the linear regime. The source class is not fixed by the hardware, but the resulting commands must lie within its frequency range, resolution and update bandwidth.

A signed 32-bit GW command is added on the 64-bit phase-increment scale. At a 125 MHz accumulator clock, one least significant bit corresponds to $125\,\mathrm{MHz}/2^{64}\simeq6.78\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

\subsection{Readout and Data Processing}

The electrical signals produced by the delay line are digitized and tracked by Moku phasemeters, with each spacecraft's phase measurements referenced to its own clock.

The synthesized links can be used directly as $\eta$ observables. Adding in the modulation, board, and readout noises, the carrier measurement is
\begin{align*}
    \eta_{ij}(t)
    ={}&\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)-\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)
    -\alpha_{ij}q_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_{\mathrm R,j}(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t)
    +b_{ij}^{\mathrm c}(t),
\end{align*}
and the corresponding upper-sideband--upper-sideband beat note as
\begin{align*}
    \eta_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}(t)
    ={}&\mathbf D_{ij}\delta\phi_j^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)-\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    +\delta\phi^{\mathrm{GW}}_{ij}(t)
    -\gamma_{ij}q_i(t)\\
    &+\nu_j^m\mathbf D_{ij}q_j(t)-\nu_i^m q_i(t)\\
    &+\mathbf D_{ij}\!\left[\nu_j^m \tau_j^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)+\psi_j^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)\right]\\
    &-\nu_i^m \tau_i^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)-\psi_i^{\mathrm{mod}}(t)\\
    &+(\nu_{\mathrm R,j}+\nu_j^m)(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i(t)
    +b_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}(t).
\end{align*}
The terms proportional to $q_i$ describe timing common to the modulation and phasemeter reference, while those proportional to $\epsilon_i$ come from the delay-line board.

The additive phase noise $b^a_{ij}$ combines optical readout, residual board, and final-phasemeter noise, in cycles; timing and modulation noise are included explicitly. To retain the noise shared by copies of a photodetector signal, write
\begin{equation}
    b^a_{ij}=\mathbf D_{ij}p_j^a-p_i^a+e_{ij}^a,
    \label{eq:shared_readout}
\end{equation}
where $p_i^a$ is the equivalent input phase noise in channel $a$ before the split, and $e_{ij}^a$ contains noise added by the individual electronic paths. This expression assumes matched tracking responses and demodulation phases, so that shared photocurrent noise gives the same phase error in each copy. Any mismatch must be included in the transfer functions rather than treating copies of $p_i^a$ as independent sources.

The effective laser phases $\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}$ enter with the same delayed-difference structure as the LISA laser phases. TDI therefore suppresses both the spacecraft and reference laser noise.

Finally, the upper-sideband-minus-carrier variable takes the form
\begin{align*}
    r_{ij}
    ={}&\frac{\eta_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}-\eta_{ij}}{\nu_j^m}\\
    ={}&\mathbf D_{ij}q_j-q_i
    +\frac{\mathbf D_{ij}m_j-m_i}{\nu_j^m}\\
    &+(1-\mathbf D_{ij})\epsilon_i
    +\frac{b_{ij}^{\mathrm{sb,+}}-b_{ij}^{\mathrm c}}{\nu_j^m}.
\end{align*}
The common GW phase cancels, leaving the clock-transfer signal together with modulation, board and readout noise.

The delay boards share an Rb reference. We write $\epsilon_i=\epsilon_{\mathrm{com}}+\Delta\epsilon_i$ to separate shared timing from differences between boards. In the constant-frequency model, the shared term is absorbed by replacing the effective source phases with $\delta\phi_i^{\mathrm{eff}}-\nu_{\mathrm R,i}\epsilon_{\mathrm{com}}$ and the clock errors with $q_i-\epsilon_{\mathrm{com}}$. It therefore has the same residual delay operator as laser phase noise in this ideal model. This identifies the algebraic cancellation, not the residual amplitude in hardware; the latter depends on the input spectrum and errors in the delays used by TDI. Differential board timing remains a separate contribution.

\section{Future Extensions}

\subsection{Changes to the Clock-Transfer and Locking Configuration}
\label{sec:clock_extensions}
Changing the modulation frequency requires separating the measured estimator into modulation, timing and readout contributions, as discussed in Section~\ref{clock noise}. A GHz implementation would also require a compatible optical and RF frequency plan. Modulating the reference laser would allow corresponding optical sidebands to beat within the electronic input band.

A second optical source and modulator on each spacecraft, together with reference interferometers and backlinks, would reproduce the local $\xi/z/\eta$ reduction and the $\beta$ clock couplings omitted here \cite{otto2012tdi}. Distinct synthesis chains and a local sideband comparison would also allow tests of differential tone-generation noise.

Backlink noise, arising from the fiber connecting the two optical benches on a given spacecraft, is similarly absent from the current configuration.
\subsection{Injection of Secondary Noise}
Test-mass displacement can be emulated by adding the corresponding phase terms $M_{ij}$ from Section~\ref{sec:modelling} to the synthesized links. Each simulated test mass must use the same displacement realization wherever it occurs, including the delayed occurrence in the reverse link. Independent noise added to every link would not reproduce these correlations. For a specified acceleration spectrum, the displacement ASD is obtained by dividing by $(2\pi f)^2$ over the analysis band. This injection would test the secondary-noise transfer functions as well as primary-noise suppression.

Independent phase perturbations can also be added after link synthesis to test a prescribed LISA-like readout-noise model. Lower optical power would test weak-light tracking and readout, but would not by itself remove the correlations introduced by splitting one detector output across boards.





\section{Conclusion}\label{Conclusion}
miniLISA combines optical laser signals, independent spacecraft clocks and electronic propagation delays to test TDI and clock correction in one measurement chain. The synthesized links reproduce the three-laser structure of the LISA measurements and a reduced sideband clock-transfer model. Adding prescribed GW phase responses allows the same experiment to test whether noise suppression preserves a known signal.

The component calculation indicates that the assumed laboratory optical powers keep detector readout below the measured electronic and modulation contributions. Electronics provide the larger measured contribution over most of the plotted band. The shared board-reference fluctuation is suppressed with laser noise, and differential timing is assumed below the included contributions. These results identify electronic noise and differential timing as priorities for characterisation, without assigning a complete sensitivity to the testbed.

The next steps are to repeat the modulation measurement with the intended source and readout, quantify differential board timing and delay errors, and compare the resulting budget with the assembled optical system. A specified GW injection will then test the recovered amplitude and phase alongside the achieved laser- and clock-noise suppression.
